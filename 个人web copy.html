Ms. Xu: Awakening the People of Hong Kong

Ms. Xu: Awakening the People of Hong Kong On the morning of April 21, 2025, at 6:00 AM, Ms. Xu began her work for the day, sorting trash in a building. Her tools were simple: a broom, a garbage bag, and an old mobile phone. The phone stored dozens of contacts, all temporary job listings---her "second job list." "Every floor here has construction waste. The renovation workers don't sort it, so I must." she said while bending over to separate broken tiles, wooden boards, and plastic films. It was her third day back at work after a two-week break due to a hand injury, but she smiled and said, "I treated it as a long vacation." At 45, Ms. Xu is one of thousands of sanitation workers in Hong Kong. They weave through the city's high-rise crevices, cleaning up its "metabolic waste," yet are rarely seen by others. "In the morning, there are inspections. If not done well, fines are imposed. But this office building is okay; most of it is cardboard, plastic, metal, nothing that gets your hands dirty." "I Know Hong Kong Best" Ms. Xu only gave me an hour for the interview. Her work area isn't limited to one building. In the morning, she sorts garbage in an apartment, in the afternoon, she might be cleaning glass in a Kowloon mall, and by evening, she's disposing of trash in an alley behind a restaurant on Hong Kong Island. Her phone's contact list is densely packed with notes like "Manager Li" and "Supervisor Wang," all part-time job agencies. "Garbage increased by 30% during the Easter holiday, but part-time work can't stop, need to save money." Her so-called "saving money" has no specific target---no children to support, no mortgage to pay, her father lives in a nursing home, and their monthly meeting meal costs less than a thousand. Yet in recent years, she feels "money doesn't last": "A bowl of wonton noodles went from 25 to 40 dollars, nursing home fees increased by 500, even rubber gloves are expensive." According to Hong Kong's Census and Statistics Department, real wages for grassroots workers in 2023 fell by 2.4% compared to 2018, while the Composite Consumer Price Index rose by 14%. Ms. Xu's monthly salary (12,000-18,000 HKD) barely exceeds the minimum wage (40 HKD/hour), but if she stops working, there's no security---she's employed by a private cleaning company and doesn't receive government benefits, with only half pay during sick leave. "When I injured my hand, I had no income for two weeks, had to dip into savings." She shook her wrist with a brace on it; now she wakes up an hour earlier than last year to work, because fast work is fast, slow work is slow, the daily workload doesn't change much. Her "part-time rush" reflects the expansion of the gig economy in Hong Kong. Amid economic downturns, companies prefer hiring temporary workers to cut welfare expenses. Labor groups estimate about 200,000 "freelancers" like Ms. Xu struggle outside the social security net across the city. "No Dreams, but a 'Running Track'." When asked about dreams, Ms. Xu shook her head: "Graduated high school and started working, no time to think about these things." Her life path is a microcosm of some of Hong Kong's grassroots: giving up further education due to family circumstances early on, having worked as a fisherman, restaurant server, supermarket stocker, and turning to sanitation work 10 years ago "because the hourly wage was as high as 5 dollars." Her place to sleep and rest is simple: a 6-square-meter partition room provided by the company, a single-person room. Her biggest entertainment is running around the housing estate's train tracks. "Can't run every day, rest when tired. Our job values physical fitness because there are regular health checks." Behind this "contentment" is the survival logic of Hong Kong's grassroots: when upward mobility is limited, lowering desires becomes self-protection. A 2022 study by Lingnan University found that the psychological health index of grassroots residents in Hong Kong is below average, but paradoxically, their "life satisfaction" is higher scholars call it "adaptive numbness." "Easter? We Only Know Garbage Volume Doubles" Holidays mean more garbage and longer hours for Ms. Xu. During Easter, mall garbage volumes soared, requiring her to work overtime handling post-party egg packaging and food scraps. "In tourist-heavy areas, trash bins fill up every half hour. We have a shift system during holidays; young people want to celebrate, they go out and play, but whether we celebrate or not doesn't matter." The Hong Kong Environmental Protection Department's report shows that in 2023, the per capita daily disposal of municipal solid waste reached 1.53 kilograms, a 23% increase from 10 years ago, but the recycling rate was only 31%. Ms. Xu's work is essentially the final step in the remedial system: "And 90% of residents throw away waste randomly, they won't sort it, we have to re-sort it at the garbage station. Holiday garbage is too much, usually three people sort it together. During Easter, I was at a mall in Tai Wai, basically guarding the trash bin because if you walk away, when you come back, the trash will overflow." Father in a Nursing Home, where is the Future? Ms. Xu's only family is her father in a nursing home. The waiting time for public nursing homes in Hong Kong is up to 3 years, so she chose a private facility for her father, costing 6,000 HKD per month. "The environment is average, but safer than living alone." Hong Kong is facing an aging crisis: 20% of the population is over 65, but elderly services are insufficient. Ms. Xu also worries about her own old age: "People like us, how dare we think about retirement? We have to work until we can't. I'll consider it when I really can't work anymore." Afterword The interview was conducted just after Ms. Xu finished her company cleaning work, and she needed to rush to Kwun Tong for her next part-time job. In this city with one of the highest per capita GDPs globally, the existence of people like Ms. Xu reminds us of the other side of prosperity: they clean up Hong Kong's glamour, yet are forced to coexist with inflation, gig work, and lack of welfare. Her view on her work is: "Someone has to sweep the garbage, right? Young people wouldn't want to do our job, otherwise how would the city function?" She paused for a moment: "This state is already very balanced; I won't complain too much." Perhaps, like the train that passes by her dormitory every day, Ms. Xu's life is just a fixed track in Hong Kong's vast system---silent, but indispensable.
